\chapter*{ABSTRACT}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Interpersonal persuasion in multi-turn natural language dialogue is characterized by fundamental communicative asymmetry: one participant acts as an arguer employing rhetorical tactics to induce compliance, while the counterparty acts as a decider evaluating concessions and formulating commitments. Standard computational models frequently treat multi-turn dialogues as symmetric or monolithic sequences, discarding functional role boundaries, suffering from tokenizer truncation, and exploiting superficial lexical heuristics in closing turns. This capstone project presents an end-to-end computational framework for modeling conversational intention and persuasion strategies across asymmetric dialogue domains.

First, we address conversational intention prediction within the \textit{Persuasion for Good} (P4G) charity solicitation corpus. Because the corpus's recorded donation fields do not reliably match what participants actually agreed to in the text, we manually annotated all $1{,}017$ dialogues and refined the labels over three dataset versions: the original binary label with a commitment modifier ($738$ yes / $279$ no), the same binary labels with the modifier dropped, and a stricter unconditional-commitment standard produced by a rule-based remap and a manual re-check of the $81$ affected dialogues ($694$ yes / $323$ no). We document fourteen successive model versions. Flat encoders limited to $256$ tokens cut most dialogues off before the closing commitment and plateau near $0.65$ Macro-F1. Hierarchical dialogue transformers ($2{,}048$-token capacity) remove this bottleneck and reach up to $0.834$ Macro-F1. To explicitly reflect conversational asymmetry, we design the \textit{AntiphonyClassifier} (v14): a speaker-disentangled, dual-stream architecture with directional cross-attention (where the decider stream queries the persuader stream) and a recurrent commitment-trajectory GRU read at the true final decider turn. Antiphony establishes project-peak test Macro-F1 scores of $0.8623$ (DeBERTa-v3-base, default threshold) and $0.8600$ (tuned threshold) on the second-pass labels, and $0.8446$ (RoBERTa-base, tuned threshold) on the strict third-pass labels.

Second, we label every Persuader turn in P4G ($10{,}600$ turns) with the persuasion strategies it uses, drawn from $41$ strategies grouped into $11$ categories; about half of all turns use more than one strategy. Because annotators do not always agree, even a perfect model could only reach about $0.79$ micro-F1 on these labels. Predicting all $41$ strategies directly proved too hard for the rarest ones, so we made the $11$ categories the main target and kept the strategies as a secondary task. An ensemble of transformer and simpler text models, combined with a calibrated averaging rule, reaches $0.7062$ micro-F1, close to that ceiling. A learned XGBoost combiner was also tried but did not beat the simple average.

Third, we investigate cross-domain generalization to conversational e-commerce negotiation by constructing a 700-dialogue benchmark through persona-conditioned LLM synthesis, organic humanization with controlled linguistic noise, and a $100\%$ manual audit. Input ablations reveal that monolithic sequence models rely on distributed merchant concessions rather than decider trajectory tracking. On an adversarially augmented corpus ($N=700$) containing non-monotonic stance reversals, the localized closing-turn control degrades to $0.9235$ Macro-F1, while Antiphony maintains $0.9619$ Macro-F1, widening the sufficiency gap from $+0.0001$ to $+0.0384$ ($+3.84\%$). Finally, forensic post-mortems resolve subtle tokenization artifacts, proving that left-truncation recovers up to $+0.1198$ Macro-F1 obscured by default right-truncation.
